How the scoring works

There is no black box. Every number on the site comes from one of four measurable signals, and you can reproduce each of them from the data in this repo.

Signal 01

Content similarity - {{ (stats.weights.content_similarity * 100)|round|int }}% weight

Each career becomes a text document built from its title, description, responsibilities, working styles and required skill names. The student's profile becomes another document, with skills repeated in proportion to proficiency so term frequency reflects strength rather than mere presence.

TfidfVectorizer with unigrams plus bigrams and sublinear term frequency, then cosine similarity across all {{ stats.careers }} careers, {{ stats.features }} features. This is the signal that lets the system recommend a role you never named, because it matches on meaning rather than keywords.

Signal 02

Weighted skill readiness - {{ (stats.weights.skill_readiness * 100)|round|int }}% weight

Not all requirements are equal. A career stores a weight per skill, from 1.0 (core, expected on day one) down to 0.2 (a bonus). Readiness is:

readiness = sum(weight * min(proficiency, target)) / sum(weight)

Skills weighted below 0.6 get a lower effective bar, because a company asking for 0.4 of a skill does not expect mastery. This is the number shown in the ring, and it is why a candidate can score well on similarity and still be "not ready".

Signal 03

Market demand - {{ (stats.weights.market_demand * 100)|round|int }}% weight

Demand is stored per career on a 1 to 5 scale and normalised. It is deliberately the smallest weight: demand should break ties between roles you are equally suited to, not push you towards a fashionable title you are not actually good at.

Signal 04

Latent factors - TruncatedSVD, {{ stats.components }} dimensions

The {{ stats.skills }} by {{ stats.careers }} skill-by-career matrix is factorised into {{ stats.components }} latent dimensions capturing {{ (stats.explained * 100)|round|int }}% of the variance. Two things come out of this:

  • Career to career similarity. Cosine similarity in latent space powers the "Similar careers" row, so a Data Scientist sees Data Analyst and ML Engineer as neighbours.
  • Adjacent skills. The average latent position of the skills you already have is matched against every career, then weighted by each skill's importance. That surface is the closest thing here to collaborative filtering over a knowledge base: it answers "what tends to sit next to what I know".

The team

Made by IGEC students

This is a major project built by students of IGEC. We wanted the kind of career guidance we never got: something that shows its working, never claims a score it cannot justify, and tells you the next single thing to do rather than a wall of links.

The knowledge base, the scoring model and the roadmap logic were written from scratch. Nothing here calls an external AI API, so your profile never leaves your own machine.

Honest limitations

  • Proficiency is inferred from text, not measured. A skill listed under Experience is stronger evidence than one on a skills list, but neither is an assessment.
  • Salaries and demand are static figures baked into the knowledge base, not live market data. Treat them as directional.
  • No user feedback loop. Scores come from content and weight only, so nothing here learns from which recommendations people actually found useful.
  • The knowledge base is small at {{ stats.careers }} roles. Content similarity degrades for careers whose descriptions share little vocabulary.
